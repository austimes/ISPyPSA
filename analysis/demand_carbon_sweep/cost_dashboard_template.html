The cost of supplying the NEM under carbon price and demand

Sixteen scenario worlds — four carbon prices crossed with four demand levels — solved as myopic pathways over 2030, 2040 and 2050 on the full National Electricity Market. This page reads one thing off that grid: what electricity costs to supply, on average and at the margin, as the two axes move. Carbon price is the dominant axis early and demand growth the steadier one; by 2050 their per-unit effects converge.

The cost surface

Average cost of supply for all 48 solved scenario-years. Each panel is a milestone year; each line is a demand level. Cost rises monotonically along both axes everywhere in the grid. The carbon price moves it most in 2030, where the fleet is cheapest to start with — $0 to $550/t roughly doubles the 2030 cost (+98% at Step Change demand) but adds only about a third by 2050 (+34%), because the higher-price worlds have already rebuilt the fleet.

What the money buys

Cost decomposition at the selected demand level

AUD/MWh

The three components sum exactly to the average cost. The carbon payment itself stays a thin, roughly constant slice once a price exists — at Step Change demand in 2030 it is 12.2 AUD/MWh at $150/t and still 12.3 at $550/t, because emissions collapse before the cheque can grow. Nearly all of the cost increase is structural: the capital and fixed costs of the rebuilt, renewable-heavy fleet.

What the marginal MWh is made of

% of marginal generation

Thermal against renewable share of the marginal generation, averaged over the three demand steps. The composition is fairly stable across years at a given carbon price (16–20% thermal at $550/t) but swings hard across prices: at 2030 the marginal megawatt-hour is 87% thermal with no carbon price and 16% thermal at $550/t. A supply block needs its own composition per price point.

The projection — (demand, renewable fraction) coordinates

An interim projection into a companion representation's coordinates

A companion representation is parameterised in (demand, renewable fraction) coordinates rather than (demand, carbon price). The panels below re-plot the same 48 solved cells in those coordinates, so the team can see where these solutions land in that space ahead of a fraction-controlled sweep.

This is a projection of price-driven solutions, not a mapping of the fraction space. Coverage is four price-traced curves per year, not a grid, and each plotted fraction is where the carbon price put it, not a target that was hit.

Coverage map — where the solutions sit in fraction space

% of generation

Every solved cell, positioned by demand level and realised renewable fraction, coloured by the carbon price that produced it. Two things to read. The slope contrast: with no carbon price in 2030 the fraction slides 16 percentage points across the demand span, because extra demand is met largely thermally; at $150/t and above the slide shrinks to 3–9 points, because most of each demand increment is met by new renewable build instead. And the empty regions: no solved cell lies between the $0/t and $150/t families — a band spanning roughly 69–84% fraction in 2030, narrowing to 84–86% by 2050 — and nothing reaches below 53% or above 95% anywhere. The blanks bound what this projection can say.

Fraction = renewable share of grid generation in this model's topology, which excludes rooftop PV. It is not on the same denominator as externally quoted shares, so no numerical comparison against authored anchors until the convention is agreed — which is also why none are overlaid on any panel in this group.

Resource cost against realised fraction

The projection of the cost surface onto the companion representation's cost-of-fraction axis: one curve per demand level, points at the four carbon prices. Resource cost is average cost minus the carbon payment. The payment is the instrument that induced the fraction and would not exist under a fraction target, so including it would overstate the cost of achieving a given fraction by the size of the cheque — about 12 AUD/MWh at 2030 Step Change demand, 7–9 by 2050.

Cost basis
Each point is the cheapest system the model found under a carbon price, not the cheapest way to reach that renewable fraction — a fraction-targeted solve could reach the same fraction at equal or lower resource cost, so these points sit on or above the true cost-of-fraction curve.

Implied fraction-cost slope: Δ cost ÷ Δ realised fraction between adjacent carbon prices at fixed (year, demand). These are arcs along the price-traced manifold, not a cost-of-fraction curve, and the denominator is small at the top of the range. — marks a suppressed arc: Δ fraction under 2 percentage points, or a cost delta inside the ±0.5 AUD/MWh noise floor; a suppressed cell is more honest than a wild ratio. Which axis matters, and how to read it

Which axis moves cost more

AUD/MWh per unit

Per-unit effects (the comparison that survives scrutiny): cost moved per $100/t of carbon price against cost moved per +10% of demand, each averaged over the other axis. Carbon dominates in 2030; by 2050 the two are equal. The full-span deltas below answer a different question — they compare the whole $550 sweep to the whole ±18% demand band, so their ratio is baked in by the choice of spans.

How to read these numbers

The acceptance record behind every figure on this page, and the resolution limit that applies to all of them (stated in full in the caveats).

Differences below the noise floor — about ±0.5 AUD/MWh on average costs, of order ±10 AUD/MWh on an arc marginal — are within solver tolerance and are not read as signal anywhere on this page.
